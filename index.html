Foundation Module

Curriculum Philosophy & Theoretical Foundations

"English is no longer a subject to be memorized, but rather a tool for thinking, analyzing, and communicating."

Egypt Vision 2030 & Secondary Transformation 🇪🇬

Aligned with Egypt Vision 2030, the new secondary curriculum fosters an autonomous, creative, and critical thinker capable of global engagement:

  • Education Quality: Elevating secondary education to align with International Baccalaureate (IB) benchmarks.
  • Inclusion & Equity: Ensuring equitable access to higher-order cognitive development across all societal segments.
  • Digital Transformation: Embedding intentional digital literacy and media criticism within daily learning.
  • Teacher Development: Evolving the educator from an instructional lecturer into an inquiry facilitator and discovery guide.

Grounded Research & Applied Linguistics

Language is acquired most effectively when embedded in authentic, inquiry-driven contexts. Drawing on Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) and Bruner's scaffolding, learning builds progressively from prior knowledge to complex academic reasoning.
Rather than discrete rule lists, the curriculum is structured around powerful, transferable concepts (Well-being, Experiences, Identity, Consumption, Sharing the Planet) enabling students to transfer understanding into unfamiliar real-world situations.
Equips learners to evaluate claims, detect cognitive bias (such as the "Highlight Reel Effect"), differentiate fact from opinion, and engage in reflective metacognitive practice.

Core Strategies

The Two Strategic Engines: JRE & IBL

Empowering learners to move from passive consumers of facts to disciplined academic thinkers.

Argumentation Framework

The JRE Strategy (Justify – Reason – Evidence)

Used to build and defend logical arguments during speaking debates and analytical writing tasks.

J

Justify (Claim / Position)

State a clear, unambiguous judgment or stance on the topic without vague generalities.

Ex: "Personal habits are the single most critical factor in determining long-term health."
R

Reason (Logical Mechanism)

Explain why your position is valid, establishing a logical cause-and-effect relationship.

Ex: "This is because daily routines directly regulate brain chemistry, sleep cycles, and stress resilience."
E

Evidence (Concrete Proof / Data)

Support your reasoning with verifiable metrics, research studies, or explicit textual quotations.

Ex: "According to WHO data in Unit 1, over 80% of inactive teenagers suffer from elevated anxiety."
Golden Student Rule: Never stop at "I think...". Always ask: WHAT is my claim? WHY do I believe it? WHERE is my textual evidence?
Inquiry Framework

The IBL Strategy (Inquiry-Based Learning)

Transforms the text from a reading comprehension exercise into an arena for critical investigation.

1. Formulating Big Driving Questions

Anchor every lesson with an open-ended, contestable question (e.g. To what extent are we truly in control of our well-being in a digital world?).

2. Detecting Media Bias & Absolutes

Train students to spot loaded intensifiers (e.g. always, completely, no excuses) and transform them into academic hedging (suggests, may indicate).

3. Evaluating Multi-Perspective Solutions

Explore balance between individual responsibility and systemic interventions (Richard Thaler's Nudge Theory in public health & food choices).

4. Teacher Guidance via Socratic Prompts

Refrain from delivering direct answers; instead, pose scaffolding questions that lead students to discover textual proof.

Classroom Execution

The 5-Period Classroom Implementation Framework

How to execute each sub-unit as an "inquiry-based continuous learning cycle" across the school week.

Period 1

Engagement + Inquiry

Student Role: Explore, predict, question

Activate prior schema via Think-Pair-Share, formulate the Driving Inquiry Question, and conduct initial Bias Discovery on visual triggers.

Strategies: IBL, Think-Pair-Share 45–50 Mins
Period 2

Listening + Evidence

Student Role: Listen, identify, extract data

Progressive 3-pass auditory decoding (Gist ➔ Evidence Extraction ➔ Analysis) and building a collaborative Evidence Board on the whiteboard.

Strategies: Active Listening, Evidence Hunt 45–50 Mins
Period 3

Language & Media Analysis

Student Role: Analyze, transform, notice patterns

Explore hedging verbs and rhetorical devices without dry grammar lectures; rewrite biased media headlines into objective academic statements.

Strategies: Discovery Learning, Scaffolding 45–50 Mins
Period 4

JRE + Speaking

Student Role: Build, defend, counter-argue

Deploy JRE orally via the Evidence Card Game, collaborative peer debates (Student A ➔ Student B ➔ Student C), and formal academic linking phrases.

Strategies: JRE Framework, Structured Debate 45–50 Mins
Period 5

Writing + Reflection

Student Role: Synthesize, peer-review, self-reflect

Architect a 5-sentence analytical paragraph (150–180 words), perform peer assessment using the CRE rubric, and complete the metacognitive self-reflection card.

Strategies: Process Writing, Metacognition 45–50 Mins
Golden Practice Tip for Educators: Never treat these five periods as disconnected, isolated lessons! They form a continuous inquiry arc. Keep the Driving Question visible on the classroom board throughout the entire week so students accumulate evidence lesson by lesson!

Grammar in Context

1. Participial Phrases (-ing / -ed):

Packing evidence efficiently: "Motivated by health goals, many individuals choose active lifestyles."

1. Gerunds vs. Infinitives:

Verbs followed by -ing (enjoy, avoid, suggest) vs. to+inf (decide, want, manage).

Extensive Reading

Extensive Reading: Teaching The Lost World

Arthur Conan Doyle's adventure adapted for Grade 11: Reading for pleasure at home, analyzing meaning in class.

The Core Professional Literature Principle

"READ (at home) ➔ INTERPRET ➔ PREDICT ➔ RESPOND ➔ ENJOY (in class)"
  • Independent Home Reading: Students read assigned chapters at home to develop reading speed and narrative immersion.
  • In-Class High STT: Classroom time is devoted to student-to-student discussion, hot-seating, and JRE justification.
  • What Teachers Avoid ❌: Line-by-line translation, lecturing, reading the full chapter aloud, over-drilling vocabulary.
  • What Inquiring Teachers Do ✅: Ask thinking questions ("Why do you think so?"), facilitate predictions, and connect themes to real-world courage.

The Lost World: Complete 10 Chapters Analytical Summary

Gladys Hungerton rejects Malone's marriage proposal, stating she could only love a man of proven bravery. Malone asks his editor McArdle for a dangerous assignment and deceives Professor Challenger by posing as a science student to secure an interview.
Challenger detects the deception and fights Malone into the street. Impressed by Malone's integrity in refusing to press charges, Challenger reveals Maple White's Amazon sketchbook, photos of the red cliffs, and a pterodactyl wing bone.

Navigating up the Amazon by steamer, canoe, and foot, the explorers reach the isolated red plateau and fell a tree bridge across the chasm. Guide Gomez, seeking revenge for his brother Pedro Lopez, destroys the bridge, trapping them atop the plateau.
The team builds Fort Challenger. Sighting living Iguanodons silences Summerlee's doubts. A violent attack at the pterodactyl nesting swamp injures the explorers, and Malone spots smoke indicating intelligent human presence on the plateau.
Seeking to prove himself, Malone sneaks out alone at night, names Lake Gladys, escapes an Allosaurus by falling into a man-made stake pit, and returns to find Fort Challenger destroyed and the professors captured by ape-men.
Roxton and Malone ambush the ape-men just as Summerlee is about to be thrown from a cliff. They rescue the professors and indigenous Accala captives, retreating to the secure cliff-caves of young Accala chief Maretas.
The explorers use their rifles alongside hundreds of Accala warriors to defeat the ape-men. The scientists observe plesiosaurs and toxodons, but the Accala refuse to provide ropes for their departure, wanting them as permanent defenders.
Challenger's hydrogen balloon experiment fails when the tether snaps. In gratitude, young chief Maretas secretly shows Malone a hidden tunnel through the cliffs. The four descend by rope to reunite with faithful Zambo and a 20-man rescue party.
In London, Challenger opens a wooden crate to release a living pterodactyl into the crowded hall, proving their discovery beyond question. Malone finds Gladys married to clerk William Potts, concluding that real courage is an internal choice rather than a prize to be won.

[Host]: Welcome to Youth & Mind: Cyberpsychology Today. Today, we examine how social media platforms have transformed from simple text messaging hubs into highly curated visual stages where adolescents perform constructed versions of their everyday lives.

[Dr. Elena Vance - Cyberpsychology Researcher]: Over the past decade, image-centric social platforms have completely reshaped how teenagers construct their self-worth. Unlike traditional television media where celebrities were clearly recognized as separate and untouchable, social media blurs the line between ordinary peers and curated influencers.

[Dr. Vance]: Psychologists term this phenomenon the "Highlight Reel Effect". Individuals selectively post only their most attractive, successful, and exciting moments while filtering out everyday challenges, fatigue, and flaws. When teenagers scroll through these feeds for hours, they instinctively treat this highlight reel as an accurate benchmark of normal life.

[Host]: And what do the clinical metrics tell us about prolonged daily screen time?

[Dr. Vance]: Recent research synthesized in clinical cyberpsychology and evaluated in JAMA Psychiatry reveals a stark pattern: teenagers who spend more than three hours a day on image-based social media platforms are twice as likely to report symptoms of low self-esteem, body dissatisfaction, and persistent social anxiety. They engage in constant upward social comparison, measuring their unedited behind-the-scenes life against someone else's polished highlights.

[Host]: So what is the solution for secondary students navigating this complex digital ecosystem?

[Dr. Vance]: The answer is not simply disconnecting from modern life. Rather, the essential armor is Critical Media Literacy. When students learn to identify biased intensifiers, recognize digital staging, and understand the language of academic hedging, they regain control over their emotional responses and self-perception.

1.1 Social Media and Self-Image

"In today's fast-changing world, social media plays a major role in shaping how teenagers think, feel, and see themselves. While it can inspire healthy habits and positive change, it can also create unrealistic expectations and pressure."

Definition: Biased language refers to words that show a strong, unverified opinion and are not fair, objective, or neutral.

Driving Inquiry Question:

"How does daily screen time on image-based apps directly affect a teenager’s self-esteem?"

Discussion in Pairs:

  • Explain: What is social media bias?
  • Analyze: How can we identify bias in social media posts?
  • Evaluate: Do you think social media has more positive or negative effects on teenagers’ self-image? Why?
1. Explain: What is social media bias?
Model Answer: Social media bias refers to the systematic skewing of reality on digital feeds, where content creators selectively publish idealized, digitally enhanced, or emotionally extreme moments while hiding everyday routines, struggles, and flaws.
2. Analyze: How can we identify bias in social media posts?
Model Answer: Bias can be identified through three key linguistic and visual markers: (a) Absolute words (always, never, completely, no excuses), (b) Emotional intensifiers (extremely toxic, ultimate perfection), and (c) Visual staging/filtering that portrays an unattainable standard as normal life.
3. Evaluate: Positive vs. Negative effects on self-image:
Model Answer: While social media provides valuable peer connection, creative inspiration, and educational fitness communities, excessive uncritical exposure creates a net negative impact on adolescent self-image due to upward social comparison and the internalization of artificial standards.

Activity 4: Language in Context – Media Analysis Table
Student Book Page 17

Study how biased absolute statements are transformed into credible, objective academic language using hedging verbs (suggests, implies, indicates, may, might, could):

Original (Biased) Revised (Neutral + Hedging)
• Everyone knows exercise is essential. • Many studies suggest that regular exercise plays an important role in maintaining health.
• Social media makes all teenagers aggressive. • Some research implies that certain types of social media content could influence aggressive behavior.
• Social media destroys teenagers' confidence. • Research indicates that excessive social media use may negatively affect some teenagers' self-esteem.
• All teenagers believe everything they read online. • Some teenagers might accept online information without critical evaluation.
• Social media is completely dangerous and should be banned for teenagers. • Social media presents certain risks that may warrant guidelines for teenagers.
1. Why replace "Everyone knows" or "All teenagers"?
Sweeping generalizations lack empirical validity. In academic discourse, replacing absolute quantifiers with quantified research phrases ("Many studies suggest", "Some teenagers might") establishes credibility and prevents unverified claims.
2. The Function of Modal Hedging (may, might, could):
Modals soften definitive cause-and-effect claims to probabilistic observations, allowing researchers to discuss risk without making unfounded universal assertions.

Activity 6: Mass Media – Social Media Post & Key Concepts
Student Book Page 18

Social Media Post for Analysis:

"I woke up at 5 am, went on a 10-km run, prepared an amazing green smoothie, and still had time for a full skincare routine. If you're not living like this, you may feel less successful than others. Every successful person I know starts the day with intense exercise and a perfectly balanced diet. There are NO excuses. Health is a choice, always."

Media Analysis: Key Concepts

Word / Expression Definition / Use in Context
highlight-reel effect The tendency to post only the best, most glamorous moments online, creating a false image of reality.
social comparison Evaluating your personal life and worth by comparing yourself to the curated profiles of others.
evaluative adjective An adjective that expresses a personal judgment, opinion, or bias (e.g. amazing, toxic, unrealistic, perfect).
1. How does the post trigger upward social comparison?
By asserting that "If you're not living like this, you may feel less successful than others", the creator explicitly equates self-worth and success with an extreme, privileged routine that most students cannot realistically replicate.
2. Identify the evaluative adjectives and absolute claims:
Evaluative adjectives include "amazing" and "intense"; absolute claims include "NO excuses" and "Health is a choice, always", which completely ignore socioeconomic, biological, and practical differences among individuals.
Activity 7: JRE Argumentation Workbench – Justify, Reason, Evidence

Prompt 1 (Realistic Picture):

"To what extent do you think social media gives a realistic picture of life? Give your claim and support it with reasons and evidence from the text."

[Evidence]: As reported in clinical cyberpsychology studies, teenagers spending over three hours daily on image feeds are twice as likely to experience diminished self-esteem due to ungrounded upward social comparison.

[Justify / Claim]: In contrast to biased social posts, the cyberpsychologist in the audio broadcast presents a highly balanced and nuanced perspective.

[Evidence]: The speaker utilizes academic hedging ("may warrant guidelines") and concludes by advocating for critical media literacy as a proactive tool to empower adolescent well-being.

4. Explain: What is the "highlight-reel effect"? Explain in your own words using evidence from the article.

1. Why are teenagers more affected by social media?
Adolescence is a crucial developmental stage of identity formation and peer validation. Teenagers are biologically and socially more sensitive to peer comparison, and their heavy screen time makes them more vulnerable to the psychological pressure of curated standards.
2. Why is social comparison considered dangerous?
Upward social comparison leads individuals to evaluate their real, flawed, behind-the-scenes lives against someone else's polished, staged highlights, creating feelings of inadequacy, anxiety, and distorted self-worth.
3. Evaluation of positive vs. negative effects:
While social media can foster supportive communities, creative expression, and educational awareness, unmoderated consumption of image-centric feeds has a net negative effect unless balanced by critical media literacy.
4. The "Highlight-Reel Effect" explained:
It refers to the deliberate practice of publishing only one's most attractive, glamorous, and successful experiences online while hiding everyday boredom and struggles, leading viewers to mistake curated highlights for everyday reality.
Activity 9: Writing in Action – Persuasive Techniques & Effects
Student Book Page 19

Types of persuasive writing techniques & their effects:

Technique Function / Purpose Examples
Rhetorical questions • Do not need an answer
• Used to make the reader think and engage emotionally		 "Do I really need this?"
"Are we performing for feeds?"
Intensifiers • Make ideas stronger and amplify urgency very, extremely, significantly, intensely
Hedging words • Show uncertainty or academic caution and prevent overgeneralization may, might, could, suggests, indicates
Absolute words • State facts with full confidence (often creating bias) always, every, never, no excuses, all
Golden Rule: "Neutral language is balanced, objective and free from bias."
Persuasive Application in JRE Academic Writing:

When crafting an academic essay or response, students must balance hedging words (to maintain scientific credibility) with strategic rhetorical questions (to introduce inquiry). Avoid relying on ungrounded absolute words, which signal personal bias rather than verified evidence.

Activity 10: Speaking – Group Discussion & Argumentation
Student Book Page 19

Discussion Questions in Groups:

• Analyze: How does the writer’s purpose affect the language choices they make?

• Analyze: Can the same information be presented differently depending on the audience? Give an example.

• Evaluation: To what extent does social media create unrealistic expectations about lifestyle and identity? Justify your position with reasons and examples from your own observations.

1. Writer's purpose and language choices:
A writer seeking to persuade emotionally uses intensifiers, evaluative adjectives, and absolute assertions to provoke rapid reactions. Conversely, a researcher informing an academic audience employs cautious hedging verbs and objective statistical evidence.
2. Audience-tailored information presentation:
Yes. For example, a scientific report on nutrition for doctors will cite biochemical metrics and controlled clinical trials, whereas a fitness influencer promoting a smoothie to teenagers on Instagram will use colorful imagery, emotive adjectives ("amazing energy"), and absolute slogans ("NO excuses").
3. Evaluation of unrealistic expectations:
To a high extent. Filtered photos, staged 5:00 AM routines, and commercial endorsements project an illusion that wealth, flawless appearance, and constant productivity are universal baselines, causing adolescents to perceive their own normal development as inadequate.

Lesson Plan: Living Well in a Complex World

Unit: Unit 1 (Living Well in a Complex World) Lesson: Lesson 1.1: Social Media and Self-Image (SB pp. 15–20) Time: 45–50 Mins Concept: Perspective, Media Literacy, Self-Perception

Driving Inquiry Question

"How does daily screen time on image-based apps directly affect a teenager’s self-esteem, and how can media literacy protect us from biased representations?"

I. Pedagogical Foundations

  • Thinkers: Critically evaluating social media bias, statistics, and media claims.
  • Reflective: Assessing their own digital consumption habits and personal self-image.
  • Contextual Relevance: Teenagers navigate image-based platforms daily. Recognizing the "Highlight Reel Effect" and biased language empowers students to protect their mental health and engage critically with digital media.

II. Learning Outcomes

  • Content Objective: Analyze how social media impacts adolescent self-esteem and identify techniques of media bias (e.g. intensifiers, generalizations).
  • Language (Receptive): Comprehend an auditory broadcast on cyberpsychology, extracting specific metrics and identifying rhetorical devices.
  • Language (Productive): Rewrite biased statements using academic hedging language and neutral phrasing; articulate arguments using the JRE method.

III. JRE+ Inquiry Sequence (Step-by-Step Implementation with Procedure Boxes)

1 Phase 1: Justification – Activate & Inquire

Time: 8–10 Mins • Schema Activation

1. Engage – Problem / Situation: Activity: Think-Pair-Share on the opening prompt: "What is social media bias, and how can we identify it in posts?" (Context: Positive vs. negative self-image effects).

2. Inquire – Question Formulation: Activity: Pose the Driving Inquiry Question: "How does daily screen time on image-based apps directly affect a teenager’s self-esteem?" Students brainstorm initial assumptions.

Teacher Action & Classroom Procedures
8 Mins (Teacher: 2m | Student STT: 6m)
  • 1
    Hook Presentation: Display the visual comparison on page 15 of the Student Book (Curated vs. Real). Prompt students: "Look at this morning routine post. Does this reflect everyday reality or an ideal?"
  • 2
    Think-Pair-Share: Give students 2 minutes of individual thinking time, followed by 3 minutes of pair discussion to define bias in their own words.
  • 3
    Central Anchor: Write the Driving Question prominently in the center of the whiteboard. Instruct students: "Record your initial opinion on sticky notes. We will test these assumptions against empirical research today."
Teacher Script / Prompts: "When you see someone posting about their 5:00 AM workout and perfect diet, what emotional reaction does that trigger? Is that post balanced, or does it show bias?"
Pedagogical Rationale (Vygotsky's ZPD): Connects abstract psychological concepts to students' lived digital realities before introducing complex academic terminology, creating emotional resonance and cognitive readiness.

2 Phase 2: Reasoning – Explore & Analyze

Time: 20–22 Mins • Auditory Extraction & Stance Analysis

3. Explore – Information Gathering: First and second listening tasks of the broadcast (The Impossible Standard: How Social Media is Reshaping the Way Teenagers See Themselves). Students complete identification questions (trends, 3-hour threshold, "highlight reel" term).

4. Think & Analyze – Evaluating Perspectives: Third listening pass (analyzing "curated stages", social comparison dangers, and statistical validity) followed by Language in Context exploration of hedging and neutral tone vs. absolute/biased claims.

Teacher Action & Classroom Procedures
20 Mins (Listening: 8m | Group Analysis: 12m)
  • 1
    Pass 1 (Gist Listening): Play the broadcast once through. Ask students: "What is the main psychological danger highlighted by the cyberpsychologist?" (Answer: Upward social comparison).
  • 2
    Pass 2 (Data Hunt): Have students complete exercise B cloze sentences on page 16. Elicit specific metrics (e.g. "Teens spending >3 hours are twice as likely to experience low self-esteem").
  • 3
    Inductive Grammar Discovery: Direct students to the "Language in Context" table (page 17). Ask: "What is the difference between 'Social media destroys confidence' and 'Research indicates that excessive use may affect self-esteem'?" Underline hedging modals (may, might, could) and reporting verbs (suggests, implies, indicates).
Teacher Script / Prompts: "Notice how the speaker avoids saying 'all teenagers are ruined'. Why does a credible scientist use words like 'may warrant guidelines' instead of 'must be banned immediately'?"
Pedagogical Rationale (Hobbs & Wineburg Media Literacy): Dual-pass auditory scaffolding trains learners to extract quantifiable data while inductive grammatical analysis equips students to decode stance and reject emotive propaganda.

3 Phase 3: Evidence – Synthesize & Support

Time: 10–12 Mins • JRE Argument Construction

5. JRE Application – Justification, Reasoning & Evidence: Students complete the Interactive Exercise: Rewrite the Bias and construct a mini-JRE paragraph addressing: "Does social media have a net negative effect on teenagers' self-image?"

  • Justify: State a clear, defensible judgment using hedged vocabulary.
  • Reasoning: Explain the underlying psychological mechanism (e.g. upward social comparison & curated highlight reels).
  • Evidence: Support with empirical data or examples from the listening text (e.g. 3-hour threshold, JAMA Psychiatry metrics).
Teacher Action & Classroom Procedures
10 Mins (Individual Writing & Scaffolded Coaching)
  • 1
    Modeling: Demonstrate rewriting a biased sentence on the board: "Teenagers waste all their time on social media" ➔ "Studies suggest that many teenagers spend substantial time on digital platforms."
  • 2
    JRE Writing Prompt: Instruct students to write a 3-sentence analytical paragraph answering the Driving Question using the JRE formula.
  • 3
    Scaffolding Support: Provide developing learners with the Hedging Word Bank (suggests, implies, indicates, may) while challenging advanced learners to integrate both a statistic and a rhetorical counter-analysis.
Teacher Script / Prompts: "Remember the Golden Rule: State your claim, explain the cause-and-effect reason, and ground it in the evidence we extracted from the cyberpsychology broadcast."
Pedagogical Rationale (Bloom's Higher-Order Synthesis): Shifts students from passive consumers of media to analytical evaluators who construct defensible academic arguments.

4 Phase 4: Reflect & Act (+)

Time: 8–10 Mins • Peer Defense & Metacognitive Transfer

6. Communicate – Present & Defend Ideas: Activity: Pair debate or small-group sharing of the rewritten neutral/hedged sentences and the identification of rhetorical questions/intensifiers from the text.

7. Reflect & Act – Transfer & Application: Activity: Personal digital reflection. Students commit to one concrete media-literacy habit for their personal social media usage over the coming week.

Teacher Action & Classroom Procedures
8 Mins (Pair Debates: 4m | Metacognitive Card: 4m)
  • 1
    Pair Review (CRE Checklist): Partner A reads their JRE paragraph; Partner B checks: 1. Is there a clear claim? 2. Is evidence cited? 3. Are hedging words used? Partners then swap roles.
  • 2
    Metacognitive Reflection: Have students fill in the Self-Reflection table on page 20: Which skill do you feel most confident in? What concrete habit will you adopt?
  • 3
    Exit Ticket Commitment: Each student submits a one-sentence personal commitment (e.g. "I will limit image app browsing to 45 minutes daily and unfollow accounts promoting toxic comparison").
Teacher Script / Prompts: "Learning media literacy is not just for an exam—it is armor for your mental well-being. How will you change your scrolling behavior tonight?"
Pedagogical Rationale (Flavell's Metacognition & Nudge Theory): Ensures authentic learning transfer by linking analytical classroom discoveries to real-world behavioral empowerment and digital well-being.

IV. Formative Assessment

1. Conceptual: Explains "Highlight Reel Effect" & media bias.
2. Critical Thinking: Evaluates statistics ("twice as likely") and challenges absolute claims.
3. Evidence (JRE): Integrates audio data and cyberpsychology frameworks.
4. Communication: Replaces absolute wording with hedging verbs.
5. Integrity: Ethically analyzes sources without unverified personal generalizations.

V. Resources & Materials

  • Audio Broadcast Script: The Impossible Standard: How Social Media is Reshaping the Way Teenagers See Themselves.
  • Student Worksheet: Section A (Social Reflection) & Language in Context exercises (SB pp. 15–20).
  • Whiteboard / Projector for modeling sentence rewrites.
  • JRE Graphic Organizer & Peer Assessment Checklist.

VI. Differentiation & Support

• Scaffolding (For developing learners): Provide a word bank of hedging verbs (suggests, implies, may, indicates) and sentence starters to assist with the rewriting exercise.
• Extension (For advanced learners): Ask students to analyze a real social media post from their own experience, highlighting hidden intensifiers and rewriting the caption objectively.

VII. Reflection & Follow-Up

Teacher Self-Reflection: "Did students successfully grasp the distinction between absolute generalization and academic hedging? Were the listening tasks appropriately paced? Follow up in the next session with a quick check on their personal digital habits reflection."
