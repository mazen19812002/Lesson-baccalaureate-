  • Digital Transformation: Embedding intentional digital literacy and media criticism within daily learning.
  • Teacher Development: Evolving the educator from an instructional lecturer into an inquiry facilitator and discovery guide.

Grounded Research & Applied Linguistics

Language is acquired most effectively when embedded in authentic, inquiry-driven contexts. Drawing on Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) and Bruner's scaffolding, learning builds progressively from prior knowledge to complex academic reasoning.
Rather than discrete rule lists, the curriculum is structured around powerful, transferable concepts (Well-being, Experiences, Identity, Consumption, Sharing the Planet) enabling students to transfer understanding into unfamiliar real-world situations.
Equips learners to evaluate claims, detect cognitive bias (such as the "Highlight Reel Effect"), differentiate fact from opinion, and engage in reflective metacognitive practice.

Classroom Execution

The 5-Period Classroom Implementation Framework

How to execute each sub-unit as an "inquiry-based continuous learning cycle" across the school week.

Period 1

Engagement + Inquiry

Student Role: Explore, predict, question

Activate prior schema via Think-Pair-Share, formulate the Driving Inquiry Question, and conduct initial Bias Discovery on visual triggers.

Strategies: IBL, Think-Pair-Share 45–50 Mins
Period 2

Listening + Evidence

Student Role: Listen, identify, extract data

Progressive 3-pass auditory decoding (Gist ➔ Evidence Extraction ➔ Analysis) and building a collaborative Evidence Board on the whiteboard.

Strategies: Active Listening, Evidence Hunt 45–50 Mins
Period 3

Language & Media Analysis

Student Role: Analyze, transform, notice patterns

Explore hedging verbs and rhetorical devices without dry grammar lectures; rewrite biased media headlines into objective academic statements.

Strategies: Discovery Learning, Scaffolding 45–50 Mins
Period 4

JRE + Speaking

Student Role: Build, defend, counter-argue

Deploy JRE orally via the Evidence Card Game, collaborative peer debates (Student A ➔ Student B ➔ Student C), and formal academic linking phrases.

Strategies: JRE Framework, Structured Debate 45–50 Mins
Period 5

Writing + Metacognition

Student Role: Synthesize, peer-review, self-reflect

Synthesize analytical thoughts into a structured JRE paragraph (100–120 words), perform rubric-based peer evaluation, and complete Exit Slips.

Strategies: Process Writing, Rubrics, Metacognition 45–50 Mins

Listening Broadcast: The Impossible Standard: How Social Media is Reshaping the Way Teenagers See Themselves
resources/00.mp3
0:00
--:--

Over the past decade, social media platforms have transformed from simple communication tools into carefully chosen stages where individuals perform carefully constructed versions of their lives. For teenagers, who are at a particularly sensitive stage of identity formation, this change carries significant psychological results.

Research published by the American Psychological Association in 2023 found that teenagers who spent more than three hours daily on image-based platforms such as Instagram and TikTok were twice as likely to report symptoms of low self-esteem and body dissatisfaction compared to those who used social media for less than one hour per day.

What makes social media especially powerful, and especially dangerous, is the mechanism of social comparison. Unlike traditional media, where the gap between ordinary people and celebrities is obvious, social media has hidden this gap completely. Teenagers are now comparing themselves not only to distant celebrities, but to classmates, acquaintances, and influencers who present their lives as easily perfect.

Psychologists use the term highlight reel effect to describe this phenomenon. What appears online is a selective, filtered, and strategically timed part of reality, yet viewers instinctively treat it as a complete and accurate picture.

Activity 1: Section A – Social Reflection & Inquiry
Student Book Page 15

1.1 Social Media and Self-Image

"In today's fast-changing world, social media plays a major role in shaping how teenagers think, feel, and see themselves. While it can inspire healthy habits and positive change, it can also create unrealistic expectations and pressure."

Definition: Biased language refers to words that show a strong, unverified opinion and are not fair, objective, or neutral.

Driving Inquiry Question:

"How does daily screen time on image-based apps directly affect a teenager’s self-esteem?"

Discussion in Pairs:

  • Explain: What is social media bias?
  • Analyze: How can we identify bias in social media posts?
  • Synthesize: What advice can we give to someone affected by unrealistic expectations?

1. Social Media Bias: The tendency of online posts to showcase an idealized, selectively filtered version of reality rather than balanced, objective truth.

2. Identifying Bias: Look for loaded emotional language, absolute terms (always, perfect, never), lack of credible evidence, and heavily edited or filtered visual content.

3. Pedagogical Advice: Practice intentional digital boundaries, curate feeds by unfollowing toxic accounts, and remember that posts represent curated "highlight reels," not full lives.

[1] My name is Edward Malone, and at the time this story began I was a young reporter on the Daily Gazette in London. I was twenty-three, full of energy, and certain that a great life was waiting for me. The problem was that I had no idea how to begin it. There was also Gladys Hungerton. I admired her more than anyone I had ever met, and one evening I finally asked her to marry me. She did not laugh at me or become angry. Instead, she looked serious and told me that she valued our friendship but did not love me in the way I hoped.
[2] Gladys said "I love a man who has done something brave. Someone who has faced danger, traveled into the unknown, and proved that he can act when it matters." I tried to joke. "So I must discover a new country before you will consider me?" "Not for me," she replied. "If a man is truly brave, he does not act just to impress someone else. He acts because courage is part of his character."
[3] Her words stayed with me. I left her house feeling disappointed, but also strangely excited. London suddenly seemed too safe. I wanted a challenge - something that would test me and give my life a clearer purpose. The next evening I went straight to my editor, Mr McArdle. He was an experienced newspaper man who had seen every kind of young reporter. When I asked him to send me on a dangerous assignment, he pushed his glasses up and stared at me. "You want danger?" he said. "Most sensible people want to avoid it." "I want a real story, sir. Something difficult."
[4] He studied me for a moment, then smiled. "Perhaps I do have a job for you. Have you heard of Professor George Edward Challenger?" Of course I had. Almost everyone in the newspaper world knew his name. Challenger was a famous zoologist, brilliant but extremely difficult. He had recently returned from South America with an extraordinary story about unknown animals. When scientists questioned him, he refused to give clear details. When reporters tried to interview him, he often threw them out of his house - sometimes literally.
[5] "Do you want me to interview him?" I asked. "If you can get through the front door," McArdle replied. "Two years ago Challenger traveled alone through a little-known part of South America. He came back claiming that something remarkable had happened. He had damaged photographs, strange sketches, and a few pieces of evidence, but he would not reveal the exact place. Most people think he is either mistaken or lying. Find out which."
[6] This was exactly the kind of chance I had been looking for. Yet there was one problem: Challenger hated journalists. If I arrived honestly and said I worked for the Gazette, the interview would probably end before it began. That night I went to the Savage Club, where many writers and scientists met. I found my friend Tarp Henry, who worked for a scientific journal, and asked what he knew about Challenger.
[7] "He is clever," Henry said, "but impossible. He argues with everyone. At a scientific meeting in Vienna he almost started a battle over a theory of evolution. If you want to speak to him, do not mention newspapers." That gave me an idea. I asked Henry to find Challenger's latest scientific paper. It was full of technical language I barely understood, but I copied one complicated sentence and used it in a letter. I pretended to be a young student who admired Challenger's work but had a question about one of his arguments.
[8] Henry read the letter and laughed. "You are taking a risk, Malone. He may realize at once that you know nothing about the subject." "I only need to get inside his study," I said. "After that, I will take my chance." Two days later a reply arrived. The handwriting looked as aggressive as the man himself. Challenger wrote that my question showed poor understanding, but he was willing to explain the matter in person. He gave me an appointment for eleven o'clock on Wednesday morning.
[9] I should have been nervous. Instead, I felt as if my real life had finally begun. On Wednesday I traveled to Professor Challenger's large house in Kensington. A quiet servant named Austin opened the door. Before he could take me to the study, a small, lively woman stopped me in the hall. "You have come to see my husband?" she asked. "Yes, Mrs Challenger." She lowered her voice. "Then I should warn you. George is a perfectly impossible person. If he becomes angry, do not argue too long."
[10] A deep voice suddenly roared from behind a closed door. Austin opened it, and I entered. Professor Challenger sat behind a wide desk covered with books, maps, fossils, and papers. He was shorter than I expected, but enormously broad. His black beard spread across his chest, and his sharp grey-blue eyes seemed to measure every weakness in me. "Well?" he demanded. "You are the young man who has difficulty understanding plain science?" I had entered looking for adventure. I had found it sooner than I expected.

Chapter 1 Summative Questions (1–7)

1. Explain how Gladys's idea of courage changes Malone's plans for his future.
Model Answer
Gladys believes true courage is an intrinsic character trait forged by facing danger. This makes Malone feel London is too mundane and motivates him to ask his editor for a perilous assignment.
2. Why does Malone ask Mr McArdle to give him a dangerous assignment?
Model Answer
Malone wants a challenge that tests him and gives his life a clearer purpose rather than remaining in the safe, comfortable routine of London reporting.
3. Explain why Malone writes to Professor Challenger as if he understands Challenger's scientific work.
Model Answer
Challenger despised reporters and threw them out of his home. Disguising his identity as an earnest science student was the only clever way to secure an in-person meeting.
4. Analyze what Mr McArdle's reaction to Malone's request reveals about Malone's attitude to danger.
Model Answer
McArdle notes that "most sensible people want to avoid danger," highlighting that Malone is impulsive, adventurous, and willing to take high risks for personal fulfillment.
5. How do Mrs Challenger's warning and Challenger's first appearance build suspense at the end of the chapter?
Model Answer
Mrs. Challenger warns that her husband is "perfectly impossible," and Challenger's broad build, roaring voice, and piercing stare create intense tension over whether Malone's fraud will be discovered.
6. Evaluate Malone's decision to use deception in order to get an interview with Challenger. Was his method acceptable? Give reasons.
Evaluation & Rationale
Ethical Angle: Deception violates pure journalistic ethics and misrepresents intent.
Pragmatic Angle: Given Challenger's violent hostility toward the press, undercover tactics were the sole practical method to investigate a story of monumental public importance.
7. Evaluate Malone's desire for adventure at this stage. Does it show maturity, impulsiveness, or both? Explain.
Evaluation & Rationale
It exhibits both:
  • Impulsiveness: Triggered rapidly by romantic rejection and an eagerness to impress a woman.
  • Maturity: A conscious yearning to test his character, conquer personal complacency, and establish a meaningful professional career.

The Lost World: Complete Novel Comprehensive Summary

A chronological overview of Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's The Lost World, mapping the full plot from the initial London proposal to the final Amazon expedition and aftermath:

Novel Arc / Stages Key Events & Plot Progression Central Characters Involved Thematic & Literary Significance
1. The Catalyst & Quest Initiation
(Chapters 1–3)		 Edward Malone is rejected by Gladys Hungerton, who demands an adventurous, heroic husband. Seeking a dangerous story, Malone accepts an assignment to interview the volatile Professor Challenger. Posing as a science student, Malone gains entry, survives a physical fight, and earns Challenger's trust. Edward Malone, Gladys Hungerton, Mr. McArdle, Tarp Henry, Mrs. Challenger, Professor George Challenger. Establishes the themes of youthful ambition, romantic delusion, journalistic ethics, and the clash between scientific truth and institutional skepticism.
2. The Challenge & Expedition Formation
(Chapters 4–6)		 Challenger reveals Maple White's sketch of a living Stegosaurus on a South American plateau. At the Zoological Society, an uproar erupts. A committee is formed to verify his claims, consisting of skeptic Professor Summerlee, adventurer Lord John Roxton, and Malone. Challenger joins them in Brazil. Professor Challenger, Professor Summerlee, Lord John Roxton, Malone. Scientific rivalry and exploration. Contrasts Summerlee's rigid skepticism with Challenger's eccentric certainty and Roxton's pragmatic wilderness mastery.
3. Journey into the Unknown
(Chapters 7–9)		 The expedition travels up the Amazon by boat and canoe. Guided by native Indians, they reach the towering red cliffs of the volcanic plateau. They fell a tall tree across a deep chasm to reach the plateau, but their treacherous guide Gomez destroys the tree bridge, trapping them in the prehistoric world. The Four Explorers, Gomez, Manuel, Zamba. The classic "Point of No Return" in adventure literature. Human vulnerability in isolated ancient ecosystems cut off from modern civilization.
4. Encounters & Survival in the Lost World
(Chapters 10–13)		 The explorers establish Fort Challenger. They encounter iguanodons, are attacked by vicious pterodactyls, and witness carnivorous dinosaurs. Malone explores alone at night and discovers Lake Gladys. A brutal war breaks out with the Ape-Men (anthropoids), who capture the professors. The Explorers, Ape-Men (King of the Anthropoids), Indigenous Human Tribe (Accala Indians). Evolutionary conflict, primal terror, and the boundary between humankind and beasts. Malone's naming of Lake Gladys highlights his lingering romantic obsession.
5. Alliance, Victory & Escape
(Chapters 14–15)		 Roxton and Malone rescue the captured professors using firearms. They ally with the indigenous Accala human tribe to defeat the ape-men, securing peace on the plateau. A grateful native boy shows them a secret tunnel through the caves, enabling their safe escape back to civilization. The Explorers, Native Indian Chief and Boy. The power of modern human ingenuity and firepower against primitive savagery, followed by the moral dilemma of indigenous tribes wanting to keep them trapped.
6. London Triumphs & Ironic Conclusion
(Chapter 16)		 At Queen's Hall in London, the skeptics denounce the report until Challenger releases a live, screeching baby pterodactyl, creating mass hysteria and undeniable proof. Malone rushes to Gladys, only to discover she married a dull clerk named William Potts. Roxton reveals rough diamonds from the plateau, and Malone vows to return to the Lost World. Challenger, Summerlee, Roxton, Malone, Gladys, William Potts. Dramatic irony and subversion of the conventional romantic quest. Malone realizes true character is built for oneself, not for the fickle approval of others.

NOVEL LITERATURE LESSON PLAN: Reader-Response & Inquiry-Based Framework

Teacher Name: Dr. Mazen Badawy
Date / Class: Secondary Advanced ELL (Grade 11)
Novel Title: The Lost World (Sir Arthur Conan Doyle)
Chapter(s): Chapter 1: A Dream of Adventure
Main Theme: Courage vs. Recklessness, Ambition, and the Search for Purpose
1. HOOK & THEMATIC CONNECTION Target Time: 5 Mins
ESSENTIAL FOCUS QUESTION / ETHICAL DILEMMA:

"Is taking extreme physical danger necessary to prove one's personal worth and true courage?"

Introduce the ethical dilemma connecting Gladys's high standards of bravery with modern human risk-taking.

2. CONTEXT & STUDENT-LED RECAP Target Time: 5 Mins
RECAP METHOD & KEY EVENTS:

Pair-Share Timeline: Students map Malone's trajectory from rejected suitor ➔ eager reporter with McArdle ➔ deceiver at the Savage Club ➔ visitor entering Kensington.

TARGET VOCABULARY / KEY TERMS (3–4 WORDS):

1. Zoologist: Scientist studying animal life and classification. | 2. Assignment: A specific, often perilous journalistic task. | 3. Deception: Using false pretenses to bypass obstacles. | 4. Evolution: The scientific theory of species adaptation.

3. ACTIVE EXPLORATION & ANALYTICAL READING Target Time: 20 Mins
ANALYTICAL READING LENS:

Track character motives (Malone's romantic vs. career ambition) and map the mounting suspense leading to Challenger's confrontation.

READING FORMAT:

Guided TTS audio read-aloud accompanied by interactive paragraph-chunking and partner note-taking.

SCAFFOLDING & TEXT-DEPENDENT DISCUSSION QUESTIONS (Bloom's: Analyze / Evaluate):
  • Analyze: What does McArdle's reaction reveal about Malone's attitude compared to sensible society?
  • Evaluate: Was Malone ethically justified in falsifying his identity in his letter to Challenger?
4. SYNTHESIS & CRITICAL REACTION Target Time: 10 Mins
STUDENT PRODUCT / REACTION ACTIVITY:

JRE Graphic Organizer & Mini-Debate: Students write a JRE response justifying whether Gladys is a positive or destructive catalyst in Malone's life, followed by a 4-minute structured stand-up debate.

5. BRIDGE & FORESHADOWING Target Time: 5 Mins
CLIFFHANGER / PREDICTION TASK:

"Challenger has uncovered Malone's superficial understanding in writing. How will the volatile Professor react once Malone speaks in his study, and how will Malone prevent himself from being thrown out?"
